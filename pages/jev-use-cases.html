Jev Use Case Takeaways
How I AI · John Lindquist · Jev use cases

27 ideas from John Lindquist and Claire Vo's Jev episode

This episode of How I AI walks through real demos built on Jev, a very fast, very cheap model from TypeSafe AI. Host Claire Vo talks with John Lindquist, creator of egghead.io, who is now building mega.dev. Their thesis: Jev outputs decisions instead of text. Its speed and low cost make model calls worthwhile for work that never justified them.

  1. Decide where Jev fits
  2. 1Reach for Jev when the choices are countable. Jev returns scores, yes/no answers and picks from a set. If your problem reduces to a limited set of actions, it fits. If you need open-ended thinking, use an LLM.Details · quote

    Many problems that feel infinite are a short list of options

    What changes. LLMs take text in and give text out. Jev takes messy text and data in and gives structured data out. That lets people talk to your functions and APIs however they like.

    Examples from the episode:

    • Web pages. A mouse can land on any pixel, but a page might have about 10 clickable buttons.
    • Games. Tetris comes down to four shape options and about 10 positions left or right.
    • 3D games. The character seems free, but the input is still the buttons on a controller.
    "whenever you have constrained inputs like that are interacting with apps it's Jev is a good thing to reach for." — John Lindquist · 26:22
    What to do with thisPick one feature and write down every action it can take. If the list fits on a page, prototype that feature on Jev first.
  3. 2Send open-ended judgment to an LLM. Critiquing a screen, brainstorming and deciding what the action should even be all stay with generative models. Check for a Jev fit only once the actions are set.Details · quotes

    Know which questions Jev can't answer

    John's contrast. Ask a model what on a web page might confuse a user. It needs to look at the image, then weigh layout, color and contrast, and discuss what it finds.

    "what on our product or website might be confusing to a user. That's not a Jev thing." — John Lindquist · 26:35

    The rough test:

    • Actions are known: look for a Jev first.
    • You don't know the action yet: use an LLM.
    • You don't know if any action is needed: use an LLM.
    "it's time to look for a Jev first I would say whereas the LLMs are much more I don't I have no idea what I want the action to be" — John Lindquist · 23:53
  4. 3Reopen the data you wrote off as too expensive to check. John and Claire both describe running models over data they would never have sent to an LLM. The cost of finding out dropped to almost nothing.Details · table · quotes

  5. 4Work through the ideas you shelved as not worth it. Claire separates two piles: new things LLMs let her create, and useful things that were too slow or costly. Jev clears the second pile, and it's the bigger one.Details · quotes

    The bigger opportunity may be the boring pile

    Why it matters. Generative models let her do things she never imagined, like building a 3D game. Jev helps with things she always wanted but could never justify.

    What to do with thisWrite down five automations you rejected on cost or speed. Rerun the math at Jev's price, and pick the one that now clears the bar.
  6. 5Treat speed as a product feature. Faster responses change how a product feels. Claire expects competition on latency to heat up.Details · quotes

    What to do with thisFind the moment in your product where people wait on a model. Ask what you would redesign if that wait disappeared.
  7. Design the decision chain
  8. 6Design with Jev by finding your if-statements. Every place code would branch is a place for a Jev call. Break the problem into those moments, then chain them.Details · quotes

    Think in conditions instead of prompts

    The mental model. Claire asked how the calls chain together. John's answer: picture a program running line by line, and mark each spot where something goes into one bucket or another.

    "just think of all the conditions the if else the the moments where it either needs to be put into this bucket or that bucket" — John Lindquist · 9:20

    The chain in his voice to-do app:

    1. Is this a valid dictation?
    2. Which task on the list does it match?
    3. Which operation does the person want on that task?

    Debugging feels different. With an LLM, John says, you often guess at a different sentence. With a decision model, you know all its options and can see which one went wrong.

    "it just feels like a function that has a lot of intelligence built into it, but it's still a function." — Claire Vo · 33:10
  9. 7Build backwards from the final action, and let scenarios expose the gaps. John started with the actions at the end, then added the steps in front of them. A strong coding model made it easy to change direction as he went.Details · quotes

    Start where the function runs

    His order. First came marking a to-do done, removing it or adding one. Only then did he add selecting a to-do from dictation.

    "I just kind of built it backwards" — John Lindquist · 9:40
    "it was so easy with the Opus 5.5 recently just to kind of wing it and like take it different directions and go through scenarios" — John Lindquist · 9:46

    His benchmark. He started from a simple question: could Jev make the to-do management in Siri or Google Assistant actually good?

  10. 8Start with the smallest demo: plain English to one function name. John's grocery demo maps "what's in my cart" to a get-cart function. Get this working before you build chains.Details · quote

    The core building block fits in one sentence

    "think of like plain English language mapped to a function name and that's one of the core foundational pieces of what Jev does." — John Lindquist · 11:29
    What to do with thisList five functions your app already has. Write three casual phrasings for each, and see whether Jev picks the right function.
  11. 9Define your classifications and APIs before you blame the model. John suspects some bad first impressions come from vague categories or weak APIs. Jev can only choose well from options that are clear.Details · quote

    The options are part of the prompt

    "this isn't good enough at classification or they don't like define their classifications or they don't have strong enough APIs" — John Lindquist · 38:24

    How firm this is. John offers it as a possible explanation for the critics, and he doesn't show a test of it.

    What to do with thisBefore a trial, write a one-line definition for each category. If two definitions overlap, fix that first.
  12. 10When Jev falls short, add another layer. John says critics often judge it on a single pass. Passes are cheap, so add one before you give up.Details · quote

    A single pass is an unfair test

    Add a pass. Classify once, then classify the result again. As you collect data on the real flows, compress those steps back down.

    "because it's fast and free don't worry about adding just another layer and then over time you could compress those down into a single layer" — John Lindquist · 38:29

    Weigh the evidence accordingly. Asked whether Jev had ever failed him, John said not yet, and noted it had been out for only days. Both speakers are enthusiastic early users. Their claims need testing on your own work.

    What to do with thisBefore your first Jev trial, write down what counts as good enough. Allow at least two extra passes before you judge it.
  13. 11Test different decision types for the same problem. Your first framing may be wrong. Try the same decision as a yes/no question, a choice between options, and a score.Details · quote

    The question format affects accuracy

    Claire's experience. A few times her first idea of how to classify something wasn't the right one. Other times she had to layer one type on top of another.

    • A yes/no question
    • A choice between options
    • A score
    "you may say, I think this is a yes no question. and you may run it and you may be like actually it's a choice question or actually it's a score question." — Claire Vo · 39:13
    What to do with thisTake one decision that's underperforming. Run it in all three forms on the same examples, and compare the results.
  14. Live voice
  15. 12Let live voice act while people keep talking. Have Jev keep asking whether it has heard enough to act. When it has, fire the function and start a fresh window of text.Details · quotes

    "Do I have enough to act?" is its own classification

    The demo. John says "book dentist appointment remove, buy oat milk complete, review pull request low priority" in one breath. He never pauses or hits enter, and the list updates as he speaks.

    "you notice that entire time I was talking I never paused. I never hit enter." — John Lindquist · 7:51

    How it works, as John recalls it. He says he can't remember the exact logic, so treat this as a sketch:

    1. Add each new word to the current text.
    2. Classify whether there's now enough to take an action.
    3. Turn the text into a payload and send it to the function.
    4. Chop off that window and start a new one.
    "it is like concatenating each word until it reaches a point where it can take an action" — John Lindquist · 35:54
    What to do with thisPick one list in your product with a few clear operations. Dictate commands at it without pausing, and count how often it acts on the wrong item.
  16. 13Use Jev as a live checklist for talks and interviews. John's presentation coach holds your talking points and checks them off as you speak. It helps anyone who goes off on tangents under a clock.Details · quote

    A coach that listens for what you still owe

    "it listens through everything I say and then starts checking the boxes to make sure that I covered everything." — John Lindquist · 34:07

    Ways to extend it:

    • John: flash a warning when time is short and points remain.
    • Claire: check off speaker notes as you go.
    • Claire: advance the slide when you've finished its points.

    Claire's test. She ran through the demo's checklist for their own conversation and found they had covered where Jev falls short only a little.

    What to do with thisPick a short checklist you use out loud, like a meeting agenda. Build this before higher-stakes voice features, since a wrong check mark costs little.
  17. 14Put a voice layer over functions you already have. The to-do list is only an example. If your calendar or other tools sit behind function calls or MCPs, the same dictation layer can reach them.Details · quote

    Dictation on top, your tools underneath

    "you could integrate your calendar into this you could integrate it because they're all behind function calls right or MCPs" — John Lindquist · 10:13

    The cost, as John sees it. He says anyone could build this cheaply, because the live inference on top costs so little.

    What to do with thisChoose the one tool you update most often by hand. List its three most common actions, and expose those first.
  18. 15Log each structured action so people can undo it. Since Jev turns speech into structured data, every action can be stored. That gives you a history, and a way to take back a misheard command.Details · quote

    Structured output makes mistakes reversible

    "it's in the history like you could have undo and whatever because they turn unstructured into structured data." — John Lindquist · 36:05

    Why it matters here. A voice app acts without waiting for you to hit enter. That makes a way back more important.

  19. Clean and group data
  20. 16Clean messy records with a confidence threshold, then audit the result. Compare records in pairs and merge only above a score you choose. Then have a smarter model review a sample of the merges.Details · quotes

    A four-step cleanup with a knob you control

    The demo. "Cedar Grove Office Products" matches "Cedar Grove Office." "Ridgeway Data" matches "Ridgeway Analytics." "Northstar Clinic" and "Northstar Clinic Services" are the harder call.

    1. Compare in pairs. Ask whether each record is close enough to another to be the same thing.
    2. Set the threshold. Choose how confident a match must be before it merges.
    3. Audit. Send a smarter LLM a random sample or the full set, and ask it to confirm the work.
    4. Explain. Have a cheap model say why each pair matched, so people can review it.
    "you want them only to merge if you're like 99% or more confident you can you can set those parameters in there." — John Lindquist · 13:57
    "you can get sort of a a quantitative matching and then a like qualitative explanation with two relatively cheap models once you've narrowed that down." — Claire Vo · 14:54
    What to do with thisExport one messy table, such as contacts or companies. Run it at a high threshold and propose merges without applying them. Review the explanations before you change any data.
  21. 17Group anything by comparing it in pairs. Claire calls this her favorite Jev use case. She used it to find which pull requests covered the same product area or theme.Details · quotes

    Pairs that used to cost too much

    "like my favorite use case of Jev, which is like pair-wise comparison of a lot of data to create grouping and clusters." — Claire Vo · 12:48
    "when you have 60,000 or 600,000 things, those pairs feel very expensive until you use something like this." — John Lindquist · 13:32

    Other targets. Claire also thinks it could have sped up cleaning about 2,000 messy entries in her password manager, using their metadata.

    What to do with thisPick a pile with no categories, such as support tickets or pull requests. Compare a sample in pairs, and see what groups appear.
  22. Routing and navigation
  23. 18Make one text box route people deep into your app. Build your smallest tools first, then add a Jev layer that picks between them. Infer as many steps as you can from one sentence.Details · quotes

    Navigation becomes a chain of fast decisions

    The demo. A user types into a command bar and can't recall what the to-do app is called. Jev still finds it. They could go further: open the to-do app and mark all the pull requests low priority.

    "you can build your smallest tools and then build another abstraction of Jev around them where it can pick which of the smallest tools to use." — John Lindquist · 16:18

    The layers can stack. The same shape applies to MCPs, with a layer that picks which one to call. John says you can build as many layers as you want.

    "route as far down the user journey as I can infer from these like structured options." — Claire Vo · 17:19
    What to do with thisWrite the 10 most common things people do in your app as function names. Test whether Jev maps plain-English requests to the right one before you build any new UI.
  24. 19Route each request to the right model and tool up front. Claire mentions people building Jev-powered coding harnesses. A fast first decision picks the model and tool, so a chain can be built and run at once.Details · quotes

    Plan the chain, then execute it

    "given the first input, let's route to the right model, let's pick the right tool" — Claire Vo · 17:31
    "I can actually just build the chain and then execute it." — Claire Vo · 17:44

    Why it helps. A slower approach takes one step, thinks, then takes the next. Fast decisions let you lay out the steps in advance.

    What to do with thisReview your recent agent tasks and sort them by the model they actually needed. Use those groups as your first routing categories.
  25. 20Reduce browser automation to the clickable elements on the page. Treat a web page as a short list of buttons instead of a screen full of pixels. That turns navigation into a fast series of choices.Details · quotes

    The page is a list once you read the DOM

    "if you actually look at the DOM there's probably like 10 clickable buttons on a page." — Claire Vo · 24:50
    "to us in our human brains, it looks like an infinite canvas of pixels, but to to a model like this, it can just be a set of decisions." — Claire Vo · 25:11

    Source of this one. Claire is explaining browser demos she has seen. John didn't show this working in the episode.

    What to do with thisTake one repetitive web task. Write the button choices at each step, and test whether Jev picks correctly from that list.
  26. 21Plan for latency in real-time search. Jev is fast but scoring every result one at a time isn't. Group results into batches, score the batches and cache what you can.Details · quotes

    The slick demos hide some architecture

    Claire's warning. The demos on social media can hide embeddings and other supporting work. She wanted to show what's underneath.

    "it actually isn't super fast to like search over all of these results and say like yes, no, this is a good result and rank it." — Claire Vo · 18:32

    Her workaround. With 4,000 YouTube comments, she grouped them into batches of about 30 to 60, then scored and ranked the batches.

    John's addition. Search is easy when everything is cached locally and you can go over the full set.

    "It is fast, it is efficient, but it's not like completely latency free." — Claire Vo · 19:05
    What to do with thisBefore you commit to a design, find a Jev search demo you like. Read its code on GitHub and see how it gets its speed, as Claire suggests.

  28. 23Give Jev a destination and let it crawl there. John's Wikipedia demo starts from any person or topic and works toward the Philosophy page. Each page is one more choice about which link to follow.Details · quote

    Goal-seeking as a series of small choices

    "mapping routes as well like giving it a final destination and it can crawl its way there." — John Lindquist · 28:56

    How the demo works. It looks at the whole page through the Wikipedia API and maps a route from the starting person to philosophy.

    What to do with thisLook for a linked data source you know well, such as internal docs. Give Jev a target page, and check whether its route makes sense.
  29. 24Check parallel agents at every step so they don't collide. Jev is cheap enough to run on every tick. Use it to steer agents just in time, instead of asking one big model for a perfect plan up front.Details · quotes

    Brute-force checking is now affordable

    The demo. A simulated workplace has three workers. The job brief: deliver the fragile blue bin to staging, bring medicine to the board and inspect the spill. Each tick, Jev moves them toward their tasks without any two landing on the same space.

    The application is still a projection. John suggests using this to keep coding agents from touching the same files. He showed the grid demo, not a working setup for code.

    "It doesn't have to plan out the entire thing ahead of time. we can do just in time." — John Lindquist · 30:06

    Claire's name for this. Mapping every route and double-checking each one is wasteful but accurate. Before, you would hand the problem to a big LLM and hope it thought hard enough.

    "Jev unlocks efficient inefficiency, which is like it is kind of like inefficient to map out all possible routes, rank them, double check that they're not going to collide." — Claire Vo · 30:28

Glossary
TermMeaning here
JevTypeSafe AI's fast, cheap model that returns decisions and scores instead of text
Decision modelJohn and Claire's term for a model like Jev, which picks from options
LLMLarge language model, the familiar kind that takes text in and gives text out
GenerativeCreating new content, such as prose, images or a game
Structured dataOutput in a fixed shape that software can use directly
Type safeThe output is guaranteed to match the format the code expects
ClassificationPutting an input into one of a set of buckets
Confidence scoreHow sure the model is about a match or decision
Function callRunning a specific piece of code, chosen from the input
PayloadThe structured data sent to a function so it can act
WindowThe current stretch of live speech being analyzed, cut off after an action
Pair-wise comparisonComparing two items at a time to decide if they belong together
DeduplicationFinding and merging records that describe the same thing
RouterSomething that sends an input to the right tool, model or screen
Multi-step classificationSeveral classification passes in a row, each narrowing the answer
Command barA text box, often opened with Command-K, for jumping around an app
MCPModel Context Protocol, a standard way to connect tools to AI agents
Coding harnessThe software around a model that makes it a coding agent
DOMThe structure of a web page, including its clickable elements
Browser useHaving a model navigate and operate websites
Work treeAn extra working copy of a code project, often left behind by coding agents
Grok BotAn agent tool John cites and Claire runs, organized around agents with roles
LatencyThe delay between a request and its response
Low reasoningA model setting that spends little effort thinking before it answers
Just in timeDeciding at each step instead of planning everything up front
