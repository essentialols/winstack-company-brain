Companies as Loops

Lenny's Podcast · Anish Acharya, a16z · Companies as loops

36 ideas from Anish Acharya on building companies as loops

Lenny Rachitsky talks with Anish Acharya, a general partner at a16z who invests in consumer companies and is a two-time founder. They cover AI fear, how companies change, model choice, consumer products and moats. His thesis: work is becoming cascading loops of agents, while people supply the intuition to find the next hill.

Get the frame right

1Stop planning around the permanent underclass.Anish calls the fear a Silicon Valley dark fantasy. He says the evidence points the other way. Put that energy into building.Details · next step · 2 clips

The evidence doesn't support the doom story

The last era centralized. Mobile-era network effects produced single winners. Now he counts about 20 relevant players at every part of the stack.

Coding agents show it. A winner-take-all market was the sensible guess two years ago. Claude Code, Codex, Lovable, Replit and Wabi are all working.

Jobs haven't followed the predictions. He jokes that radiologists have been doomed for about 20 years. He says postings for them and for programmers are at record highs.

The labs describe something milder. He says insiders see autocatalytic effects, where AI improves the process. That falls short of true recursive self-improvement.

2Ask whether your problem is really limited by intelligence.Many problems are bound by something other than brainpower. More intelligence won't fix those. Spot the difference before you bet on AI.Details · next step · 2 clips

A data center of PhDs won't dominate pizza

His test case. Put a data center of PhDs at FedEx or Domino's. He doubts they would exponentially dominate supply chains or pizza.

Competition may barely move. If Pizza Hut, Domino's and Papa John's all adopt AI, he expects some shuffling. He doesn't expect one of them to hold 99% of the market.

Diffusion is slow too. He visited his small hometown last summer and found lives hadn't changed much. Model progress is fast while economic spread lags.

Separate the marketing from the real risk

One announcement can hide several motives. He names three that can sit behind holding a model back.

  • Marketing: the aura of having a model too dangerous to ship
  • Capacity: a shortage of GPUs for inference
  • Economics: keeping an advantage internal instead of selling it

The risk he accepts. He says we should harden our systems before models make them trivial to break into.

Leads haven't lasted. He notes Anthropic looked unassailable, then OpenAI had an amazing six months. Open-weight models are moving fast as well.

8Write each function as a loop from input to outcome.A bug report can become a shipped fix in five minutes. Anish expects the same shape in marketing, sales, support and legal.Details · steps · next step · 3 clips

From prompts to agents to loops to cascades

The progression. We had prompts, then agents, which are models in a loop with tools, memory and skill files. Loops are sets of agents doing tasks.

Coding shows the pattern first. It has the best models, the most technical customers and established loops. Here is his bug-fix example.

  1. A bug report comes in.
  2. A repro gets generated.
  3. A fix gets created.
  4. The fix gets reviewed.
  5. High risk: a human confirms before production. Low risk: it just ships.
  6. Maybe the customer gets an email saying it's fixed.

The cascade. He names four levels: person, job function, business unit and large parts of the company. In the strong form, a loop tells the CEO that the business model or strategy must change.

What to do with this

Pick one recurring workflow. Write its trigger, steps, risk check and finish line. Mark the step that still needs a person.

9Run growth experiments as a loop.Replace the experiment war room. Generate every variant, measure it, converge at significance, then start the next.Details · steps · next step · 1 clip

The war room becomes a machine

The old way. The team gathered, listed possible experiments, prioritized them, then built and shipped them.

The loop version. He describes it in five steps.

  1. Generate every variant.
  2. Measure every variant.
  3. Once results reach statistical significance, converge and ship the winner.
  4. Keep a long-term holdout.
  5. Start the next experiment.

Know the limit. The loop climbs to a local maximum, then plateaus. That is your cue for new human thinking.

Hear it in the episode
  • 14:43 The growth team as a loop

What to do with this

Automate one experiment type end to end, such as email subject lines. Review it when it plateaus, instead of at every step.

The compounding part. The call unblocks the agent right away. The agent also captures the traces and learns from them.

Choose models

14Match model spend to the job's upside.Unbounded upside justifies frontier prices. Bounded jobs want good performance at a good price. Expect both inside one company.Details · table · next step · 3 clips

Frontier tokens for some jobs, mid-IQ for others

Frontier pricing looks irrational. He estimates that one conceptual IQ point costs 100x more going from Opus 4.8 to Fable 5.

The exception. In drug discovery, that point could find the next statin, a trillion-dollar outcome. There, the premium makes sense.

Bounded work. You can't close the books 100x better. Those jobs should sit on the efficient frontier, often with open-weight models tuned by reinforcement learning.

JobUpside, as he frames itModel tier
Drug discoveryUnboundedFrontier
Sales, support, research, engineeringHighFrontier
Legal, financeBoundedMid-IQ, often open-weight

Two arguments against a split. A support call may hold the breadcrumb that changes the company, which argues for frontier everywhere. Or we've already crossed the useful threshold for almost everything.

34Treat growth problems as product problems, and run the Birkin exercise.Anish says nobody has a growth problem these days, only a product problem. Imagine your product at $1,000 or $10,000 a month.Details · steps · next step · 2 clips

Price is a measure of product-market fit

Old wisdom reversed. Consumer products were supposed to be free. He says every part of consumer discretionary spending is now up for grabs.

The exercise. He frames it as a software Birkin bag.

  1. Imagine your product costs $1,000 or $10,000 a month.
