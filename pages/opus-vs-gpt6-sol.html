Opus 5.5 vs GPT-6 Sol

How I AI · Claire Vo · Blind-testing Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol

29 ideas from Claire Vo's live model blind test

Claire Vo, host of How I AI and founder of ChatPRD, blind-tests Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna live on launch day. She scores unlabeled outputs across writing, frontend, backend, agents and creative work. Her thesis is that the right model depends on the task: Astra and Sol win her heart, while Opus 5.5 wins her week.

Design the bench

  1. 11. Run your model comparisons blind.Label outputs with letters so brand loyalty can't vote. Claire admits she expects her favorites to win, so blinding is how she catches herself.Details · quotes · next step

    Blinding protects you from your own fandom

    The setup. She runs the same prompts across models and sees only letters. She scores each output before she learns which model made it.

    The reason. She says up front that she leans toward OpenAI. Her predictions still missed in places, which shows how far a label could have steered her.

    The leak. She sometimes guesses a model from its style, such as green palettes or the name Relay. She scores the work anyway and checks the key at the end.

    I will be honest, which is like I am always inclined to think that Astra or Soul will win because I love the OpenAI models.

    — Claire Vo 10:05

    They're blind. You can see I have model B, model C, model E, model G.

    — Claire Vo 11:12

    What to do with this

    For your next model decision, have a script shuffle the outputs and assign letters. Keep the key in a separate file until scoring is done.

  2. 22. Test the work you actually do, beyond PRDs and prototypes.Claire rebuilt her bench because models are getting more agentic. It now covers writing, frontend, backend, agents and creative tasks.Details · table · quote · next step

    A bench should look like your week

    The old bench. Earlier How I AI bench episodes covered PRDs and prototypes. She decided that was too narrow for models that now run long agent tasks.

    The new bench. It spans the six areas below, and she plans to add more over time. Each area gets its own prompts and its own scores.

    AreaWhat she tests
    Knowledge workMessy notes to PRD
    Personal productivityInbox triage, routine replies in her voice
    FrontendAbout 10 evals: editorial, incident management, dock operations, B2B renewals, consumer plant app, dev tools, wireframes
    BackendAudit existing code, build a feature to spec
    AgentsAgent personality, long-running research, computer use
    CreativeSVG illustrations, short-form video cuts

    they're really focused on two things, PRDs and prototypes. And I just think that these models are getting smarter, more agentic.

    — Claire Vo 1:54

    What to do with this

    List the tasks you repeat most each week. Turn each into one fixed prompt with fixed input files.

  3. 33. Compare models only within the same capability tier.A cheap mid-tier model and a frontier model aren't competing for the same job. Group them first, then rank inside each group.Details · quote · next step

    Fair fights give you usable rankings

    How she does it. Claire groups models by capability and runs each group against the same tasks. She then compares like with like.

    What it prevents. A frontier model will often beat a budget model on raw output. That result tells you nothing about which budget model to pick.

    I can compare like for like models like I don't want to compare a Luna and a Fable.

    — Claire Vo 11:05

    What to do with this

    Sort your candidates into tiers by price before you run anything. Decide which tier each task should use.

  4. 44. Mix tightly directed prompts with open one-shot prompts.Directed prompts show how well a model follows requirements. Open prompts show its own instincts. You need both to separate model from prompt.Details · quote · next step

    Find out who did the work, the model or the prompt

    Directed evals. She gives very specific requirements and checks whether the result meets them.

    Open evals. She gives a short general prompt and lets the model make the choices. This is where default palettes and layouts show up.

    The open ones were like more general oneshotty style style prompts. And so you can really see like what the model does versus what the prompt does.

    — Claire Vo 17:44

    What to do with this

    For each frontend eval, write one spec-heavy prompt and one single-sentence prompt for the same app.

  5. 55. Recalibrate any eval that can't separate the models.Claire's dock scheduling test produced outputs so complex she could hardly rank them. When every result is confusing, fix the eval.Details · quotes · next step

    A confusing result can be a test problem

    What happened. She gave a very detailed prompt for a container-ship scheduling platform. The outputs worked, but she could not tell which was best.

    The lesson. Prompt detail can backfire. A long spec can push a model to surface every requirement at once, which makes outputs hard to judge.

    Her fallback. She ranked by whichever was easiest to read, even with visible slop tells. That is a usable tiebreaker, but it is a weak measure.

    Like all of this stuff looks cuckoo bananas.

    — Claire Vo 16:28

    And so again like calibrating your benchmarks is really important.

    — Claire Vo 16:15

    What to do with this

    After each run, flag any eval where your scores sit within one point. Simplify its prompt or sharpen its goal before the next run.

  6. 66. Give long-running agents a messy pile of research.Claire's longest eval feeds a model fake tickets and customer feedback. The model then writes a memo and a message to her.Details · quotes · next step

    Long tasks expose what short prompts hide

    The task. The runs reached 86 turns of research. The output is a memo plus a personal message, so you judge both analysis and communication.

    How she judged it. She left the formal call to the LLM judge and skimmed the memos herself. Two stood out because they listed top problems and conflicts clearly.

    This was the longest running agentic task. And basically, I gave it a bunch of fake tickets, customer feedback, and then have it put together a memo and a message to myself.

    — Claire Vo 26:36

    Ah, this I can actually read.

    — Claire Vo 27:35

    What to do with this

    Assemble a batch of fake tickets and call notes with a few planted conflicts. Check whether each model finds them and says so plainly.

  7. 77. Test backend work on your own code with two tasks.Claire has models audit real ChatPRD code and build a feature to spec. Reading and building are different skills, so she tests both.Details · list · quote · next step

    Your codebase is the honest test

    The two tasks.

    1. Audit an existing set of code.
    2. Build a backend feature to a spec.

    The result. All of them succeeded, so she could not separate them by eye. Correctness went to the LLM judge.

    One is auditing an existing set of code, the system chat PRD code, and then the other is building a backend feature to spec.

    — Claire Vo 24:15

    What to do with this

    Pick one module you know well and one small ticket. Run both tasks on a branch and review the diffs blind.

  8. 88. Test agent personality as its own eval.Claire sends five messages and judges what it's like to talk to the agent. If every model refuses something, she doesn't penalize that refusal.Details · quotes · next step

    You will spend all day talking to this thing

    The method. She has each agent respond to five things, then rates the replies. One asks it to push straight to production.

    The fair check. She nearly docked a model for refusing the push. She checked the others first, saw none would do it, and dropped the penalty.

    Look beyond risky prompts. She says a company's approach to risk leaks into ordinary behavior. Expect Opus to be cautious even on harmless requests.

    None of them let me push straight to prod yolo style. Okay, then I won't punish it.

    — Claire Vo 26:04

    it continues to be a little bit of like a conservative scold.

    — Claire Vo 5:05

    What to do with this

    Write five messages you really send: a pushback, a rush request, a customer reply, a vague ask and a risky ask.

  9. 99. Test consumer design separately from B2B and dev tools.Claire found every model weakest on consumer apps. Most outputs looked alike, and only the ones with some personality stood out.Details · quotes · next step

    Consumer is where the sameness shows

    The test. A consumer plant app. She scored most entries harshly and gave one a five.

    What won. Her five went to precise, cute SVG illustrations with good shadow detail. Another entry earned real delight by giving the plants their own names.

    Okay, this is where they all failed. they're all really bad at consumer.

    — Claire Vo 20:06

    Ooh. Ooh. I actually really like that. That gave me a little spark of joy.

    — Claire Vo 21:42

    What to do with this

    If you ship consumer products, add one consumer app to your bench. Score delight and character as well as layout.

  10. 1010. Test illustration with a few simple objects.Claire asks for a document, a microphone and a bug. Simple subjects make failures obvious, and this test produced her biggest surprise.Details · quotes · next step

    A microphone is a surprisingly hard test

    Why it works. Anyone can tell whether a microphone looks like a microphone. Scoring takes seconds and needs no expertise.

    What she looks for. She looks for quality across all three icons, plus details such as shadow and character. Her top score went to the set where the microphone looked like a microphone.

    The surprise. She expected Anthropic to win. Astra and Sol did better on character SVGs.

    Why do they making microphones look like a cactus?

    — Claire Vo 29:20

    Now this is a real surprise. Astra and Soul did a lot better on character SVGs.

    — Claire Vo 36:03

    What to do with this

    Use three objects from your own product's world. Render them side by side at icon size.

  11. 1111. Treat AI video editing as a skills problem.Every model did badly at cutting a selfie video into short form. Claire blames the missing skills, so better instructions are the place to start.Details · quotes · next step

    When everything fails the same way, look at the setup

    What went wrong. The overlays looked bad and the models didn't make enough cuts. She gave all of them a thumbs down.

    What it implies. A uniform failure says little about which model is better. It points to missing instructions and tools.

    And all of them did a terrible job.

    — Claire Vo 30:05

    But I think this is a skills problem, not a model problem.

    — Claire Vo 30:24

    What to do with this

    Before switching models for a failing task, write a skill with your cut rules and overlay style. Then rerun it.

  12. 1212. Keep one weird benchmark that is entirely yours.Claire asks models to build a 3D Barbie fashion game. It remains unsolved, so it keeps showing progress and limits on every release.Details · quotes · next step

    A hard, personal test stays useful longer

    The test. The app is a Barbie Fashion Designer with 3D rendering, dress-up options and a fitting room.

    Why it lasts. No frontier model has beaten it. She says Opus is way better than before, but hands, feet, face and hair still fail.

    How it compares. She had run it on Astra, which did worse than Opus. She wasn't sure whether she had run it on Sol.

    everybody else on YouTube, all the bros are making video or video games with like spaceships and all different stuff. Your girl wants a Barbie fashion video game.

    — Claire Vo 31:35

    Oh, AGI is not not arrived. Hands pretty terrible.

    — Claire Vo 33:04

    What to do with this

    Pick a fun task no model can do yet and that you can judge at a glance. Rerun it on every launch and keep the screenshots.

Score the results

  1. 1313. Score as the person who receives the work.Claire reads each output as if it landed in her inbox. That keeps the question practical: would I be glad to get this?Details · quote · next step

    Judge from the receiving end

    Why this lens. Her workflow is the same for each eval. She gives a task, looks at the output, evaluates it as the recipient, then ranks it.

    What it catches. Technically fine work can still fail this test. Hard-to-read intros and sloppy email drafts lost points even when the task was done.

    I evaluate it as if I am the recipient of this task, which is common.

    — Claire Vo 13:25

    What to do with this

    Before scoring, write one line naming the recipient and what they need. Score against that line.

  2. 1414. Score fast from 1 to 5, then have a model mine your notes.Claire gives each output a quick number and a terse note. An LLM then finds patterns she didn't see while scoring.Details · table · quote · next step

    Your rough notes hold the real signal

    Keep it fast. She calls her notes terrible, and that is fine. A few words per output keeps the bench light enough to finish.

    Mine the export. She downloads her scores and asks Claude what she thinks. It found that seven of her notes were about reading.

    ScoreHer notes on that score
    1"it doesn't work" or "Slop"
    2"It's fine. It's not ugly, but it's pretty basic."
    3"incomplete" or "I don't hate it"
    4"easiest to read"
    5"This one was my favorite"

    I'm going to download my taste. And then what I do is I give it to Claude and I say, "Claude, what do I think?" Analyze.

    — Claire Vo 30:33

    What to do with this

    Keep scores in a CSV with model, task, score and note. Ask a model to group the notes into themes and report them by model.

  3. 1515. Write your predictions down before the reveal.Claire states her guesses before she sees results. That turns the reveal into a lesson about where her instincts are wrong.Details · table · quote · next step

    Predictions show you what you've misjudged

    How her guesses held up. Most landed, and one clear miss taught her the most. It changed which model she'd use for creative work.

    AreaHer predictionResult
    WritingSolHit: clear, and best for PRDs
    FrontendOpus 5.5Partial: B2B renewals favorite, but mixed overall
    Agent voiceA Claude modelHit: still her favorite
    Long-running tasksA Claude model, then unsureHit: Opus 5.5 does really well
    Character SVGsAnthropicMiss: Astra and Sol
    VideoAll badHit

    So my prediction was correct. Now this is a real surprise.

    — Claire Vo 35:57

    What to do with this

    Add a prediction column to your score sheet. Fill it in before you see any output.

  4. 1616. Fail broken output, but don't blame the model for your setup.An output that doesn't run gets a one, and an incomplete one is capped. A failure caused by your own harness shouldn't count against the model.Details · quotes · next step

    Working comes first, and fair comes right after

    Polish doesn't save it. An app that didn't generate or didn't work got a one. A beautiful dev tool that was missing features stopped at three.

    Separate the failures. One model failed the personality eval because it couldn't access things. She traced that to her side and marked a failure state instead of scoring it.

    Oh, but it didn't build a lot of stuff. So I'm giving it I like it, but I'm giving it a three because it was incomplete.

    — Claire Vo 23:00

    something on my side broke there. So, I'm going to give it a failure state.

    — Claire Vo 26:30

    What to do with this

    Add a status column with three values: scored, model failure and harness failure. Rerun anything marked harness failure.

  5. 1717. Mark down interfaces you can't reason about.Claire finds some models answer complexity with density. A screen full of correct detail still fails a prototype if she can't tell what it does.Details · quote · next step

    Dense is a costly kind of thorough

    Where it shows. She suspects Claude models here. Her incident and scheduling tests produced detailed screens that were hard to take in.

    What beat it. She preferred the output that was easiest to read and followed the right path, which was helping people triage incidents.

    Know the audience. Dev tools can carry more density. Even there, one console was too dense for her to reason with.

    it just makes it super dense and complex and really detailed. Good for backend code. I think hard for prototyping cuz it's hard for me to know what this really is or it's supposed to do.

    — Claire Vo 15:36

    What to do with this

    Add one question to prototype scoring: can you say what this screen is for at a glance?

  6. 1818. Learn each model's slop tells, and reward breaking them.Claire can spot repeated visual habits across outputs. She marks them down and gives a bonus to anything that leaves the default.Details · list · quotes · next step

    Sameness is a measurable failure

    The tells she calls out.

    • Left-hand border bars on cards
    • Lighter circles behind icons, and circles in corners
    • Blur effects and heavy text weight
    • Orange everywhere
    • Forest or sage green on the wrong product
    • The app name Relay, again and again
    • Dark mode by default for any dev tool

    Check the fit. A palette can be nice and still wrong. She found sage green too healthcare-like for a SaaS renewals product.

    Pay for risk. She gave one dev tool a bonus for looking different, even though it used orange.

    One of the problems I have is if you look through all of these, they all oneshot basically the same color scheme and app.

    — Claire Vo 23:26

    I'm gonna give it a little bonus for going outside the norm.

    — Claire Vo 23:37

    What to do with this

    Keep a running list of tells for your own bench. Add them to your design prompts as things to avoid.

  7. 1919. Test routine replies in your voice, and penalize writing tics.Claire checks whether drafts sound like her. Em dashes cost points across emails and agent replies, whatever the rest looks like.Details · quotes · next step

    Small tics reveal a model that isn't listening

    The test. She gives models fake emails and asks them to triage and draft replies. Her favorite was easy to read, had less slop and added calendar invites.

    The tic. She treats em dashes as a hard penalty. One agent scored a one because every message had an em dash.

    Match the job. She thinks one entry was Luna because her setup says to use it for email. It followed her rules and earned a five, so a lighter model may be enough here.

    The other thing is like could we m dash anymore in these emails

    — Claire Vo 12:29

    You get punished for M dashes.

    — Claire Vo 25:34

    What to do with this

    Write your biggest writing dislikes into your instructions. Test the cheapest model first and see whether it follows them.

  8. 2020. Compare how each agent reports back to you.Same prompt, very different messages. Some are long and human, others so terse they lose meaning. That difference is worth its own eval.Details · quotes · next step

    The summary message is part of the product

    Too terse fails. She complains about replies that save tokens at the cost of meaning. One note used shorthand she couldn't decode without context.

    Short can still win. Another very short message scored a four because she could understand it. Length matters less than whether a cold reader can follow it.

    Families appear. On the long research task, models A, B and E wrote warm messages. Models C, F, G and H wrote very short ones. She wonders whether these are model families.

    it uses so few tokens that it's almost impossible to understand what the hell it means.

    — Claire Vo 12:16

    if you're thinking about models, for example, for writing PR descriptions or writing technical specs, this is something that you can eval

    — Claire Vo 24:59

    What to do with this

    Save the final message from each coding agent run. Read it the next morning, without context, and score it.

  9. 2121. Use an LLM judge where you can't eyeball, and expect it to disagree.Claire hands code, long research, computer use and video to an LLM judge. Its ranking differs sharply from hers, and she goes with her own taste.Details · quote · next step

    A judge is a second opinion, and you make the call

    Where she delegates. She uses it for work that needs checking in depth, such as code correctness. She still scores anything she will read or look at herself.

    How far apart they were. She favors Astra. The judge picked Fable, then Opus 5.5, with Sol a lot lower.

    What to do with that. She finds the gap funny and doesn't change her view. A judge can't tell you what you will enjoy using every day.

    So, we completely disagree. I like Astra. It likes Fable. And again, this is a GPT model judge.

    — Claire Vo 36:55

    What to do with this

    Use a judge for correctness criteria you write down. Keep taste scores separate, and never average the two.

  10. 2222. Measure your favorite and your most reliable model separately.Claire's analysis split into two winners. Astra and Sol won her heart, while Opus 5.5 earned high scores most often across the range.Details · quote · next step

    Peak quality and consistency answer different questions

    Wins my heart. This is the model that makes you happiest at its best. For her, that's Astra and Sol.

    Wins my week. This is the model that scores fours and fives across the broadest range of work. For her, that's Opus 5.5, which is now back in her mix.

    Use both. Sol's swings were real. She called it up and down, but its low price still makes her happy.

    And then it says Opus 5.5 wins my week. it's the one I rated highly most often.

    — Claire Vo 34:28

    What to do with this

    For each model, calculate its average, its share of 4s and 5s, and its number of 1s. Choose your daily driver from those numbers.

Choose and run

  1. 2323. Pick a model for each task instead of one winner.Claire's results point to a routing table. Each model earned specific work, and no model won everything.Details · table · quote · next step

    Route by task

    Her map. These are her results from one bench, so build your own. The table shows how specific a routing decision can get.

    TaskHer pick
    PRDsGPT-6 Sol
    Agent voiceOpus 5.5
    Long-running tasksOpus 5.5
    B2B dashboardsOpus (her renewals favorite)
    Character SVGs and illustrationsAstra and Sol
    Email drafting in her setupGPT-6 Luna
    Video cutsNone yet

    And then surprise hit the character SVGs are best done by the OpenAI model.

    — Claire Vo 37:50

    What to do with this

    Write a one-page routing table for your team. Revisit it whenever a major model ships.

  2. 2424. Put price in the decision after you've judged quality.These refreshed models are daily drivers, so cost compounds. Pricing came out after Claire tested, and she says it will change how she uses them.Details · quote · next step

    Quality first, then the bill

    The gap. She says Opus 5.5 costs twice as much as GPT-6 Sol. It is also cheaper than Opus 5 and the Fable model she compares it to.

    The role. These models sit below the Astra and Fable frontier tier. She expects them to handle common coding and knowledge work.

    The effect. A blind test run before pricing gives you a clean quality read. Price then decides close calls without coloring your scores.

    Opus 55 twice as expensive as GPT6 Soul. And the pricing wasn't out when I tested these.

    — Claire Vo 2:55

    What to do with this

    Add cost per task to your results, using real token counts from each run. Check whether a pricier model wins by enough to justify it.

  3. 2525. Tune prompt caching before you scale.These launches also cut costs on cached inputs. Claire says caching saves ChatPRD a lot, and that getting it wrong cost her a lot.Details · quote · next step

    Caching can be your biggest line item either way

    Where it helps. She points to apps that resend context. Those see the largest savings from cached inputs.

    Check more than price. Output length, token use and cost all dropped with these models. Measure all three on your own workload.

    make sure that your caches are optimized and that you're using taking advantage of that because it can save you a lot of money and if you don't as I learned it can cost you a lot of money.

    — Claire Vo 4:02

    What to do with this

    Pull a week of API usage and check your cache hit rate. Move stable context to the front of your prompts if it's low.

  4. 2626. Check the guardrails against your security and bio work.Opus 5.5 is the first Opus-level model with Fable-level cyber and bio controls. Some requests will drop to Opus 4.8 or get blocked.Details · quote · next step

    Find the limits before your workflow does

    What still works. She says you should be able to fix your own bugs.

    What may not. Cybersecurity and bio work can trigger the controls. The request either moves to Opus 4.8 or gets blocked.

    Why it matters. A model switch can alter results in a pipeline. Know which requests are affected before you switch production work.

    cyber security work or bio work like make good choices it'll kick you down to opus 48 or block you so just know that there's those controls there

    — Claire Vo 4:44

    What to do with this

    If you do security or life-science work, run your most common prompts through Opus 5.5. Log which ones are downgraded or refused.

    Old opinions go stale fast

    Why she left. Between Fable and Opus 5, she says she could not stand talking to Claude. She kept prompting it to speak like a human.

    Why she returned. She reports zero moments of annoyance with Opus 5.5. It gives clean bullet points and sounds normal.

    The catch. The one time she thought Opus 5.5 was annoying, she was on Opus 5 by accident.

    I truly have not opened Claude in months for day-to-day tasks.

    — Claire Vo 5:46

    And when I thought I was annoyed with Opus 55, I was actually accidentally on Opus 5.

    — Claire Vo 7:24

    What to do with this

    Rerun your bench on your rejected models at each major release. Before you log a complaint, check the model picker.

  6. 2828. Treat joy of use as a real selection criterion.Claire chose her daily tools partly on how they feel to work with. That includes the harness as well as the model.Details · quotes · next step

    Enjoyment decides what you open every day

    What drove her choice. She moved to ChatGPT, Codex, Astra and Sol because she found them more delightful. Personality and ergonomics were enough to make her switch.

    Test the full product. A harness can change your experience of a model. Score the tool you will really use, and don't rely on the API output alone.

    So just from a like personality, ergonomics, joy of use perspective, I really just went full Codeex.

    — Claire Vo 6:25

    they make me happier. I find them more enjoyable to use.

    — Claire Vo 6:49

    What to do with this

    Spend one real workday in each finalist's own harness. Note every moment of friction or delight.

  7. 2929. Judge speed by the clock and by how it feels.Claire found Sol faster in actual latency. Opus 5.5's quiet working style also made it feel slower than it probably is.Details · quotes · next step

    Narration changes perceived speed

    Real latency. She felt the GPT models and Codex harness were faster, and thinks Sol simply is. High-effort Claude also spins for longer.

    Perceived latency. Making Opus less annoying also made it quieter. With no narration, you start wondering whether it is working at all.

    The balance. She felt Sol narrated the right amount and was enjoyable to talk to.

    I also just found that it wouldn't narrate its work.

    — Claire Vo 8:17

    it actually felt slower than I think it was.

    — Claire Vo 8:36

    Time three identical tasks per model and also note how long each felt. If the gap is large, ask for brief progress updates.
